# MuoviTech video content and knowledge value

Prepared by Codex on 11 September 2026 from the local Teams and SharePoint material in `C:\Mouvitech-project`.

## My assessment

**The material has useful value beyond asking a general AI model, but that value is concentrated in the product study, its discussion, and the pressure-drop demonstration.** The introductory course mostly teaches established geothermal engineering. It provides a consistent training resource, while the other recordings preserve specific results, practical assumptions, customer examples, commercial considerations and qualifications that a general explanation would miss.

The distinction is not simply public versus secret. There are three separate questions:

1. Is the information general engineering or specific to MuoviTech?
2. Is it public, commercially sensitive, or of uncertain publication status?
3. Can a model answer accurately and traceably without the source material?

A public research result can be highly valuable to retrieve accurately. An internal recording can contain mostly public teaching material. A confidently stated meeting opinion can also be wrong or incomplete. These distinctions matter when deciding what belongs in a useful company knowledge base.

My strongest conclusion is that the project should preserve **MuoviTech's specific evidence, decisions and reasoning**, with general engineering material supplying the background needed to understand them.

## What the videos cover

### 1 Geothermal technologies and market context

The first training module provides a map of the geothermal field. It distinguishes shallow systems using heat stored near the surface from deep geothermal resources, and separates direct extraction from deliberate thermal storage.

It introduces soil collectors, groundwater systems, rock boreholes, surface-water systems, borehole thermal energy storage, aquifer storage, energy piles, and larger pit or cavern storage. It explains why the appropriate technology depends on the building, geology, hydrology, temperature requirement and project scale. Energy piles are presented as a way to use building foundations as part of the energy system.

The temperature ranges and market figures provide context for sales and onboarding. The lecturer discusses the growth of geothermal use, the differing roles of direct heat and ground-source heat pumps, and Sweden's strong position in European heat-pump installations. These are statistics presented in the recording, not current market figures independently checked for this report.

**Knowledge gained:** staff can explain what geothermal energy includes, recognize the principal system types, and place MuoviTech's collectors within that wider field. This is primarily general industry knowledge.

Sources: part 1, `tr-01-000` at 00:00:01 through `tr-01-006` at 00:08:28.

### 2 Ground properties and their effect on design

The second module explains the two main ground properties used in the course: undisturbed ground temperature and thermal conductivity. Warmer ground and better heat transport can reduce the required borehole length for a given example; colder or less conductive ground can increase it.

The lecturer explains seasonal temperature changes near the surface, the more stable temperature deeper down, the geothermal gradient, and how buildings and urban surfaces can alter the ground temperature. She also connects rock composition and moisture to heat transfer: quartz-rich rocks generally conduct well in the examples, while adding moisture improves the performance of soils.

Illustrative calculations show the sensitivity of drilling requirements to ground conditions. In one example, moving from 9 °C ground to 2 °C requires nearly twice the drilling. These figures belong to the teaching example and should not become universal sizing rules.

**Knowledge gained:** staff can explain why the same building load does not imply the same drilling requirement in every location, and why a site assessment matters before recommending a collector system.

Sources: `tr-02-000` at 00:00:00; `tr-02-003` at 00:04:16; `tr-02-005`–`tr-02-008` at 00:07:06–00:11:14.

### 3 Borehole heat exchangers and thermal resistance

The third module introduces the physical borehole assembly: borehole, casing, collector pipes, filling material, bottom weight and well top. It explains the terminology used for collectors, probes and borehole heat exchangers.

It compares single-U, double-U and coaxial arrangements. The central concept is borehole thermal resistance: the temperature difference needed to transfer heat through the fluid boundary layer, pipe wall and surrounding filling material.

The course uses a theoretical example to show how lower resistance reduces the fluid-to-borehole-wall temperature difference. It then explains why a design with attractive heat transfer is not necessarily the best overall choice: production cost, transport, installation and reliability also matter.

The full meeting and later tool demonstration qualify the comparison. A double-U arrangement may have lower resistance under favorable flow conditions, but splitting the flow between more circuits can make it laminar and undermine that advantage. The answer therefore depends on the operating condition, not just the collector category.

**Knowledge gained:** staff can explain why collector selection requires both thermal and practical reasoning, and why “double is always better” is not a defensible general recommendation.

Sources: `tr-03-000`–`tr-03-008`; particularly 00:06:02–00:11:56. Qualifications: `tr-09-046` at 01:05:23 and `tr-12-021` at 00:35:39.

### 4 Grout groundwater and active borehole length

The fourth module explains why a borehole needs thermal contact between the pipes and the borehole wall, and why grout also serves a sealing function. Sealing prevents unwanted movement between groundwater layers or entry of surface water.

The lecturer contrasts grouted installations with groundwater-filled boreholes in suitable Nordic geological settings. Although stagnant water is not an exceptional conductor, movement and natural convection can improve effective heat transport. This distinction between material conductivity and heat transported by moving water is useful for understanding borehole performance.

The module covers bottom-up grout injection, partial seals, and active borehole length. In the groundwater-filled example, the air-filled section above the water table does not provide the same useful thermal contact as the submerged section.

It also distinguishes diffuse groundwater movement, fracture flow, artesian flow, thermosiphon effects and convection inside the borehole. Country rules mentioned by the lecturer describe her account at the time; they are not a current regulatory assessment.

**Knowledge gained:** staff can discuss why filling choices vary between projects and countries, why drilled depth differs from active depth, and how groundwater can affect performance.

Sources: `tr-04-000`–`tr-04-010`, especially 00:01:10, 00:03:59, 00:05:11 and 00:09:20–00:13:43.

### 5 Heat carrier fluids and flow conditions

The fifth module compares glycols, alcohols and salts as heat-carrier fluids. It discusses viscosity, heat transport, pumping effort and practical considerations in fluid selection, including ethanol mixtures used in Sweden.

The main lesson is the tradeoff between heat transfer and pressure drop. Laminar flow is relatively easy to pump but mixes poorly. Turbulent or unsteady flow improves mixing and heat transfer but generally requires more pumping effort. Reynolds number provides a way to relate flow, fluid properties and pipe dimensions.

The module connects this to TurboCollector: its internal fins can maintain improved mixing at lower Reynolds numbers than a smooth pipe in the cited simulations. It also explains why adding parallel flow paths or increasing pipe diameter can lower pressure drop while creating a less favorable flow regime.

Seasonal temperature behavior is discussed, along with a rule of thumb that a 1 °C change in heat-carrier temperature affects heat-pump heating capacity by roughly 3–4%. That teaching statement concerns **capacity**, and should not be confused with the separate COP claims in the product material.

**Knowledge gained:** staff can understand why selecting the lowest-pressure-drop option alone may produce a poor system, and why fluid and seasonal operating conditions matter to the product's benefit.

Sources: `tr-05-000`–`tr-05-010`, especially 00:06:09–00:12:12 and 00:13:40–00:14:53.

### 6 Freezing and diagnosing a cold borehole

The sixth module distinguishes freezing in grout from temporary freezing around pipes in a groundwater-filled borehole. The lecturer explains how freezing can damage grout contact or its sealing function, and why some temporary freezing in water-filled Nordic installations is treated differently.

The useful content is the discussion of limits and failure mechanisms: permanent freezing in an undersized system, trapped water between ice plugs, pipe deformation, and ice formation around long steel casing in fine soils.

The diagnostic examples distinguish low average fluid temperature from an abnormal temperature difference between inlet and outlet. A cold system with the expected temperature difference may suggest insufficient ground capacity; an unusually small or large difference can point toward flow regulation or another equipment problem. The lecturer explicitly presents these as theoretical illustrations, not a universal diagnostic procedure.

**Knowledge gained:** staff can ask better troubleshooting questions and avoid assuming that every low-temperature complaint means the borehole is too short.

Sources: `tr-06-000`–`tr-06-008`, particularly 00:03:46–00:06:41 and 00:08:11–00:10:34.

### 7 Borehole spacing thermal interference and recharge

The seventh module explains how heat extraction changes ground temperature over time and how neighboring boreholes influence one another. It introduces superposition: the temperature effects from several boreholes combine.

The lecturer presents approximately 20 m spacing as a practical convention for reducing interaction between separate systems, explicitly qualifying that it does not mean perfect thermal independence. This matters because the same spacing rule is not appropriate for every borehole field.

Options discussed include angled drilling, greater depth and recharge. In a field deliberately designed for thermal storage, interaction can be useful. The module also emphasizes annual net heat extraction as an important driver of long-term thermal influence.

**Knowledge gained:** staff can explain why depth, spacing, energy balance and field layout belong in the same design discussion, and why adding boreholes is not simply adding independent capacity.

Sources: `tr-07-000`–`tr-07-007`, especially 00:04:07–00:07:48.

### 8 Thermal response testing

The final short module explains how a thermal response test measures the ground's response to a known heat input. The purpose is to estimate effective ground thermal conductivity, with borehole resistance and undisturbed temperature also obtained from the test interpretation.

It describes the test equipment and the temperature curves used to interpret results. The lecturer specifies at least 60 hours in her description, explaining why a short test may mainly reflect the borehole rather than the surrounding ground. This is the procedure taught in the material, not a newly verified universal standard.

The full meeting adds useful qualifications. Testing should represent the design flow conditions; laminar conditions can complicate interpretation. Experienced operators can recognize signs of groundwater influence in the curves. Distributed TRT uses depth-resolved temperature measurements to investigate variations and possible fracture zones.

**Knowledge gained:** staff can explain why larger projects need measured site information, what a TRT contributes, and why interpretation requires expertise.

Sources: `tr-08-001`–`tr-08-005`; additional discussion in `tr-09-082`–`tr-09-085` at 02:05:16–02:09:29.

### 9 Additional knowledge in the full introductory meeting

The full recording is not a second independent course. Its additional value is the questions asked by the MuoviTech audience and the lecturer's responses.

The first Q&A connects regional drilling practice to grouting pressure, installation constraints and the permitting thresholds described by the lecturer. It clarifies that poorer ground conductivity can require more total drilled metres even where practical constraints favor shallower individual boreholes.

The discussion of SDR classes connects pipe-wall thickness to resistance and installation pressure. Importantly, the lecturer declines to invent a percentage improvement for changing SDR without doing the calculation. Another exchange distinguishes manufacturer sizing tools for small systems from more detailed borehole-field design tools for larger installations.

The final Q&A covers identifying an existing heat-carrier fluid from documentation or professional sampling, testing at representative flow conditions, interpreting groundwater effects, and distributed TRT. Some questions remain unresolved or are deferred. These boundaries are themselves useful knowledge: a chatbot should preserve them instead of supplying an invented answer.

**Knowledge gained:** the course is connected to the team's actual questions, including the points that require calculation, additional information or a specialist.

Sources: `tr-09-039`–`tr-09-047` at 00:54:52–01:07:04; `tr-09-080`–`tr-09-086` at 02:02:41–02:11:02.

### 10 The TurboCollector study and its qualifications

The Niklas Hidman recording explains the research behind the fin design. It uses Direct Numerical Simulation to examine heat transfer and friction under specified conditions. The method resolves fluid and thermal behavior without turbulence closure models; it remains a numerical simulation with assumptions and a defined scope.

The presentation discusses validation against smooth-pipe analytical solutions and correlations, grid refinement, and a structured comparison of fin strategies, heights, counts and twist rates. More than 30 designs were evaluated, including preliminary tests.

The selected promising design has alternating helical fins, 0.6 mm height, 16 fins and 360° twist over about 0.7 m. The modeled reference uses a 40 mm inner diameter. The reported advantage is concentrated in the transition region, approximately Reynolds 1700–2300, where the research deck reports a 300–600% increase in **convective heat transfer** relative to a smooth pipe. That is not a 300–600% improvement in heat-pump efficiency or annual energy use.

Outside that region, the reported friction difference is small. The later discussion mentions a roughly 2% difference at a particular data point, rather than establishing a universal penalty for every configuration.

The Q&A provides some of the most valuable qualifications in the entire folder:

- The researcher explicitly says he cannot call the selected geometry the optimum, only one of the best tested designs.
- A combination of two fins, 45° twist and 0.6 mm height was not tested as that exact combination. Results from separate parameter variations cannot simply be combined into a new proven design.
- Changing fluid changes the applicable Reynolds and Prandtl numbers. The benefit must be interpreted at the actual operating condition.
- Splitting flow through a double collector can move the system below the useful operating window.
- Improving the collector does not automatically justify reducing the system flow. The heat pump and the rest of the system also matter.
- Participants discuss possible further research and acknowledge that they lack the whole-system knowledge needed to promise an improvement from tuning existing installations.

The recording also includes country-specific production variants, material usage, pricing concerns, competitor opinions and sales preparation. Those should be separated from the scientific findings. Statements about the competing “shark” collector are participants' interpretations, not an independent competitor test supplied in this folder.

**Knowledge gained:** staff can explain the product's supporting evidence, the relevant operating window, and the limits of the claim. This is considerably more useful than retaining only the headline “up to 600%.”

Sources: `tr-10-006`–`tr-10-019`; `deck-s25`–`deck-s33`; `tr-10-024` at 00:32:28; `tr-10-027` at 00:36:04; `tr-10-037` at 00:48:02; `tr-10-043`–`tr-10-046` at 00:55:33–00:59:15.

### 11 The pressure drop tool and customer design reasoning

This recording combines a practical lesson in hydraulic design with a demonstration of the tool available at the time. Its title card dates it to **19 February 2026**.

It explains pump curves, available pressure and the loss of flow when a circulation pump cannot overcome the system resistance. Inputs include borehole and pipe lengths, diameters, SDR, fluid, flow and the number of parallel circuits.

Worked examples show why adding a parallel borehole at unchanged total flow reduces pressure drop, why unequal branches need balancing, and why the highest-resistance branch determines the required pressure in the demonstrated balanced system. They also show why a larger diameter can solve a pressure problem while creating an unfavorable Reynolds number.

One customer example uses eight 250 m boreholes, 40 mm pipe, 10 m connections to the manifold, 50 m of 110 mm main pipe, and two heat pumps requiring a combined 5.19 L/s. The stated target is about 65 kPa. The presenter changes the pipe sizes and discusses the operating benefit of TurboCollector at part load.

Two inspected screenshots corroborate the pipe-sizing example: **159.1 kPa at 00:32:00** and **63.37 kPa at 00:34:00**. The visible collector and horizontal-pipe diameters change from 40 to 50 mm; TurboCollector is enabled in both frames. The pressure reduction therefore must not be attributed solely to switching on the TurboCollector option.

The presenter describes the customer conversation as taking nine minutes and approximately doubling the contribution margin on the collectors while improving the proposed design. This is a recorded commercial anecdote, not independently verified evidence of the chatbot's return on investment. The exact second amount and currency are not clear enough to reproduce as confirmed financial figures.

The 4×32 comparison explains the difference between **physical boreholes and hydraulic circuits**. Eight physical boreholes with double-U collectors create 16 parallel loops. Halving depth to 125 m while retaining the same total physical drilling requires 16 boreholes, giving 32 parallel loops with double-U collectors. Dividing the flow between all those loops can make the Reynolds number too low. The demonstrated single-U alternative uses 16 physical boreholes and 16 circuits. Calling the tool's 32-circuit input “32 physical boreholes” would misrepresent the example.

Several operational details are especially valuable:

- Pipe lengths are entered **one way**, not as the sum of flow and return.
- The presenter describes a **2.5 kPa U-bend allowance** and a **10% margin** associated with pipe-production tolerances and additional uncertainty.
- Manifold and valve losses are not explicitly modeled and must be considered separately.
- The demonstrated tool supports only **one manifold chamber**, not arbitrary multi-manifold networks.
- In the 60 kW heat-pump example, the required flow is **3.1 L/s** regardless of whether the collector is smooth, turbo or double-U. This is a particular example, not a universal flow specification for every 60 kW heat pump.
- The tool can export a PDF with results and a material list.

The Q&A also records the release process: initial availability on the group and Swedish pages, a planned week of internal testing, feedback to Michael, and broader publication afterward. A heat-pump selector was withheld pending permission to use manufacturer data. Per-section pressure losses, clearer instructions and a single/double selector were discussed as improvements. Those statements describe February 2026 status and plans; this review does not establish which features exist today.

**Knowledge gained:** staff can follow the team's design reasoning, enter the demonstrated tool correctly, understand its omissions and recognize when a case exceeds its scope. This is one of the strongest sources of practical additional value.

Sources: `tr-12-000`–`tr-12-024`; tool assumptions in `tr-12-029`–`tr-12-034` at 00:48:56–00:56:50; rollout and feedback in `tr-12-025`–`tr-12-036`.

### 12 Narration and supporting documents

The short TC narration packages the product explanation for a sales audience: improved mixing at reduced flow, increased convective heat transfer, and benefits over a long service life. Its main value is the chosen explanation and wording, rather than new scientific evidence.

The local presentation and marketing script add the headline chain: up to 22% lower flow at transition, up to 80% reduced fluid resistance, up to 3.6 °C higher brine temperature, and up to 11% COP improvement. These quantities describe different parts of the system. They should not be treated as interchangeable, simultaneous guarantees for every installation, or measured annual energy savings.

The supporting slides also give product-range information: collector dimensions, SDR choices, lengths, high-temperature MuoviXpert applications, cabinets, chambers and related components. These are specifications stated in the supplied presentation, rather than a checked current catalog.

The correlations presentation adds fitted heat-transfer and friction-factor expressions, including their Reynolds-number regions. It is valuable reference material for technical implementation, but any equation used in a calculation should be checked against the original slide and its stated domain rather than copied uncritically from extracted text.

## What a normal model can answer and what the sources add

A capable general model can already explain Reynolds number, why turbulence improves mixing, what a TRT measures, and the tradeoffs between common collector arrangements. Retaining the videos is not necessary merely to make those explanations possible.

The added value appears when a question requires the **specific source rather than a plausible general explanation**:

| Question | What the material adds |
|---|---|
| What fin geometry did the study actually evaluate and select? | Exact parameters, with the distinction between a promising design and a proven optimum. |
| Can we combine the two-fin result with the 45° result? | The explicit statement that this exact combination was not tested. |
| Does a low-pressure-drop double collector always outperform a single collector? | The recorded flow-dependent comparisons and their limitations. |
| Does the tool include manifold and valve losses? | A recorded explanation of what is omitted and what the consultant must consider separately. |
| Should I enter 100 m or 200 m for a 100 m connection? | The one-way input convention taught in the demonstration. |
| Does TurboCollector mean an existing heat pump should run at lower flow? | The distinction between collector behavior and the heat pump's required flow, plus the acknowledged need for whole-system analysis. |
| Why was a tool feature missing at the time? | The actual release discussion, such as awaiting permission to use manufacturer data. |
| Was there a decision to add plain water as an option in the tool? | No such decision is established in the reviewed tool recording. Water physics discussed elsewhere must not be presented as that decision. |

For the original comparison request, a no-material model run was completed before a separate source-backed analysis. It gave substantive answers to five general engineering questions, but did not supply the requested exact study figures or recorded meeting details. That supports my conclusion that the sources add useful evidence for these selected questions. It does **not** show that the facts are absent from all models, that none is public, or that a model allowed to retrieve the public study would fail.

The question set was deliberately chosen, and this was not a blinded engineering benchmark. It also does not establish the deployed chatbot's answer quality: supplying the full material to an assessment is different from retrieving only a few passages for each chat question.

## Where I would focus the knowledge base

I would retain the general course for onboarding, but prioritize the following material for answer quality and business usefulness:

1. **Research claims and their conditions.** Keep the exact finding, operating range, comparison and caveat together. Do not separate a headline number from its qualification.
2. **Tool operating instructions.** Capture the length convention, circuit counting, included allowances, omitted losses and supported system layouts as clear, dated entries.
3. **Worked examples and decisions.** Preserve the inputs, the problem identified, the change made and the reason. These show employees how the team applies its knowledge.
4. **Questions and limits.** Preserve statements such as “not tested,” “requires calculation,” and “needs whole-system understanding.” These help the chatbot recognize when it should stop and refer the question to an engineer.
5. **Commercial context with appropriate access.** Keep pricing, material usage, margin discussion and competitor opinions distinct from public product explanations. Recording a commercial opinion does not validate it as a technical fact.

To determine actual business value, the next useful test is a representative set of employee questions, scored by an engineer without knowing which answer came from which system. Compare an ordinary model, a model using public product/research sources, and the internal knowledge base. Measure correctness, unsupported claims, appropriate abstention, source traceability and time saved.

**My recommendation is to keep developing the knowledge base around the product evidence, tool behavior and recorded expert reasoning.** The folder supports a substantial training resource and a useful internal reference. It does not yet establish a precise percentage of exclusive knowledge, verified annual product savings, current status of every tool feature, or a measured financial return from the chatbot.
